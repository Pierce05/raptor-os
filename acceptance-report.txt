Design decisions made seeding the real fixture, defended here and in
JUDGING.md:
  - The real fixture has no rubric at all. Scoring dimensions
    ("functionality", "quality", "innovation") only exist implicitly
    as the union of criteria keys used across the 126 score entries.
    seed.py collects that union and creates one RubricCriterion per
    name, weighted equally. The alternative (guessing organizer-
    intended weights) has no basis in the data; equal weighting is the
    only assumption the fixture itself actually supports.
  - The real fixture has no organizer account, and no passwords for
    judges or team members at all. One organizer account is
    synthesized; every real judge/team-member email is given a single
    shared, fixed seed password (see SEED_PASSWORD in seed.py) --
    these are fixture/test identities with no real credentials to
    begin with, not real people's accounts.
  - No explicit assignment records exist in the fixture. A judge
    scoring a project implies they were assigned it; seed.py creates
    one Assignment row per unique (judge, project) pair appearing in
    `scores`. No additional assignment-only rows were fabricated --
    the fixture's own uneven review counts (some projects have 2
    scores, others 5) are already the "awkward case" data; inventing
    more would misrepresent the source file.
  - judge_a/judge_b were chosen as the fixture's first two judges
    (judges[0], judges[1]) rather than arbitrary/synthetic accounts.
    judge_a (Tomas Varga) happens to have exactly ONE score in the
    real file -- a genuine, non-contrived example of the sparse-judge
    shrinkage case documented in JUDGING.md's normalization write-up.
  - participant was chosen as the first member of the fixture's first
    team (a real team, NorthKiln, with a real submitted project), so
    the T1.3 deadline-rejection check exercises "team exists, event is
    closed" rather than an uninteresting "no team yet" 400.
